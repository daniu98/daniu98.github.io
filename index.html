01About

How I play

I like problems where the answer has to survive contact with the real world. A model that has to make a call in three milliseconds on a drone. An API that has to make sense of prerequisite text written by 141 different departments. A robot that has to find its own way out of a maze with nothing but two IR sensors and whatever I wrote the night before.

Hockey is where I learned how to work. Ten years of it — early ice, positional reads, trusting four other people to be exactly where they said they would be. The habits carry over more than people expect: see the whole sheet before you move, say the thing early, and when the shift is ugly, finish it anyway.

Lately that looks like document transfer infrastructure on AWS, a course-planning backend for UCLA students, and two years of research on low-latency models small enough to run on the edge. I'm happiest somewhere between the systems and the people who have to use them.

Read the play. Take the shift. Leave the team better.

02On the ice

Ten years on the ice

Hockey isn't the thing I do when I'm not working. It's where most of what I know about teams, preparation and losing well actually came from.

Competitive play

Over ten years of organized hockey, from learn-to-skate through competitive teams. Most of it has been spent on the details nobody applauds — gaps, supports, getting back.

Leadership

I've held leadership roles on teams I've played for. The job was rarely the loud part of it: set the tone at practice, keep the bench together after a bad period, and make sure the newest player on the roster feels like they belong on it.

Coaching & access

I coach beginners — a lot of them stepping onto the ice for the first time. Hockey is an expensive, intimidating sport to walk into, and I've spent real hours working to make it less so for the people coming in behind me.
